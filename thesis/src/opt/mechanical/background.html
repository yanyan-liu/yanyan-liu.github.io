Background¶

While OPT achieves high-resolution 3D imaging [102, 103], reconstructions often suffer from artifacts that impact their quality due to model mismatches. Among them, mechanical errors play a non-negligible role, especially for low-cost OPT systems [102].

Previously Reported Artifacts and Correction Techniques¶

The most common type of mechanical error is an offset of the rotation axis which results in a misaligned center of rotation during the experiment. The consequences depend on the direction of this translation mismatch. A constant shift in the direction parallel to the detector plane has been reported in both OPT [58, 104] and X-ray CT [105]. It will cause a constant shift in 2D projections, generating ring artifacts: a point in the sample will be imaged into a circle after reconstruction. For example, Tang et al. reported circles in imaging a zebrafish embryo [104]. Walls et al. observed a so-called double-edge artifact while imaging the cardiac region of a mouse embryo [58] and Donath et al. observed tuning-fork artifacts of a point object [105]. A shift in the direction of light propagation will result in out-of-focus blur due to limited depth-of-field that is unique to OPT [106].

Different methods are available to correct for an off-center rotation in both OPT and X-ray CT. Walls et al. proposed a heuristic method that calculates the variance of each reconstruction of a 2D slice using a range of assumed shift values in a sinogram: the shift value that produces the maximum variance serves as a close guess for the true shift [58]. This method is fast to implement and yields satisfactory reconstructions when the volume is not too sparse; it is commonly used as a preprocessing step by OPT practitioners [13]. Other methods such as sinogram unification of both fluorescent and bright-field OPT [104], center of mass or image registration [105], total-variation regularization [98], and methods that take advantage of the symmetrical structure of certain samples [103] are also used in OPT but with limitations because they require a priori information. These 2D methods assume that the tilt of the rotation axis is negligible. Their extension to small tilt angles introduces a height dependence of the rotation axis [93, 102].

Tilt of the rotation axis in 3D can be described by a combination of out-of-plane and in-plane tilt. Only in-plane tilts of a small angle have been studied. With this assumption, each transverse slice of the sample still approximately corresponds to a row of pixels on the camera; then, compensation of 3D tilt reduces to a 2D problem of finding the true center of rotation for each slice. This is done using the same techniques as for dealing with off-center rotation. For example, Torres et al. [93] used maximum-variance method [58] to find the rotation center at two different heights. They then performed a linear regression to obtain the shift of rotation center at any intermediate horizontal plane. For larger tilt angles, a method to account for tilt in 3D is still missing.

Another type of mechanical error that has not been well studied is the angular errors of the rotation motor, which will result in seagull-shaped artifacts for point-like objects, as we will show in Catalog of Mechanical Artifacts. While many efforts have been made to characterize and correct mechanical artifacts, they focus on a single type, whereas a comprehensive documentation of all sorts of mechanical artifacts did not exist.

Contribution¶

We rely on a 3D description of the imaging system that uses a set of angular and translational parameters to systematically document many mechanical artifacts. We provide a catalog of artifacts which lists their cause, resulting effects, and existing correction methods. It serves as a reference for OPT practitioners and assists them to calibrate their experiments.

We introduce a joint reconstruction-calibration algorithm that is able to recover the unknown set of characterizing parameters at coarse scale. Refined system parameters are then fed into the 3D reconstruction algorithm to achieve a distortion-free volumetric image at a finer scale. We validate our algorithm on simulated beads data and experimental data of a fluorescent textile fiber.
