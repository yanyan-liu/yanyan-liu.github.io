Appendix: Cone-Beam Shift of Origin due to Off-Center Rotation¶

While we have shown that the RI mismatch transforms the parallel problem into a conic one, the center that yields the optimal FDK reconstruction is not the \(y_0\) predicted by Eq. (36), but rather an \(y_r\) that is significantly closer to the object, with \(y_0>y_r\). Following recent evidence regarding the effects of recalibration on seagull artifacts [13], we have established experimentally that a mis-centering of the cylinder with respect to the axis of the turning platform is at the origin of this offset.

To reconcile the conic geometry motivated in Eq. (36) with this newly characterized experimental error, we proceed to show that the offset of the center of rotation accumulates the original projections in a way that generates an alternative cone with its center displaced towards the origin. Indeed, even when misaligned, the platform still rotates the sample fully, and hence, generates complete tomographic data. The center of a cone beam corresponds to the position through which all light rays pass; we thus look for the point where the flux of intensity is maximal.

The effect of the rotation on the \(y\) direction being negligible because \(y_0 \gg R\), we focus instead on the movement induced in the \(x\) direction. This displacement can be expressed as \(r\cos \theta\), where \(\theta\) is the angle of rotation and \(r\) he distance between the center of the sample and the true axis. At each angle of rotation, the rays originate a light cone that is viewed from \(y_0\) with an angle \(\psi\), which in turn depends on \(R\) (Fig. 20). These cones accumulate on the line segment \([-r,r] \times \{y_0\}\) (blue in Fig. 20) according to the amplitude of rotation.

Let us establish the coordinate system \((x,y')\) centered on \((0,y_0)\) and with the axis of symmetry flipped, so that \(y=(y_0-y')\). In the \((x, y')\) system of coordinates, the finite domain limits the spatial influence of the rays, making them all coincide at \(x=0\), \(y'=y'^\star = r \cot \left( \psi /2 \right)\), and thus creating an equivalent cone center under fairly general conditions. To show this, we use \(f(x,y')\) to model the brightness within the span of each cone and \(w(x)\) to weight their contributions in a way that reflects how the projections concentrate at the poles. Together, they multiply to yield the weighted intensity flux

(38)¶\[g (x, y') = f \left( \sqrt{x^2+y'^2} \right) w(x).\]

All contributions can then be combined by integrating over the \(x\) movement as

(39)¶\[\begin{split}I(y')/2 = \left\{ \begin{aligned} & \int_0^r g\left( x, y' \right) \,\text{d}x, \quad y' \geq y'^\star \\ &\int_0^{y'\tan(\psi/2)} g\left( x, y' \right)\,\text{d}x, \quad y'<y'^\star, \end{aligned} \right.\end{split}\]

which is split into two components precisely because the offset rotation has a finite amplitude. Note that the weighted intensity energy \(I\) is continuous as \(y'=y'^\star\) is the solution to \(r=y'\tan(\psi/2)\), and that the factor of 2 appears due to the symmetry of \(g\) with respect to the integration domain.

The maximum of \(I\) is attained at \(y'^\star\) when \(g\) is constant, thus originating a (uniform) cone beam closer (\(y'^\star>0\)) to the object. We now show that such a maximum still exists under quite general physical conditions. On one hand, brightness is to be conserved, one expects the divergence of \(f\) to vanish in the radial direction \(\rho\) of the corresponding polar reference system, yielding that \(\nabla \cdot f = \rho^{-1} \partial \left( \rho f \right) / \partial \rho=0\) and, thus, that \(f \propto \rho^{-1} = 1/\sqrt{x^2+y'^2}\). On the other hand, the weighting function can be thought to be proportional to the derivative of the geometric projection of the rotation, like \(w\propto \left|\sin (\pi x/2r) \right|\). Together, \(f\) and \(w\) combine into a \(g \propto \sin (\pi x/2r) / \sqrt{x^2+y'^2}\) that makes \(I\) monotonically increasing for \(y'<y'^\star\) and decreasing for \(y'>y'^\star\) because the integration domain shrinks progressively. Therefore, with the maximum \(y'^\star=\text{argmax}_{y' \in [0, \infty)} I\), this showcases the formation of an equivalent cone beam that is closer to the object. With respect to the original system of coordinates, the shifted cone-beam center is at \(y_r=(y_0-y'^\star)\).

To find the center \(y_r\) of the cone beam in practice, we resort to the automatic calibration scheme presented in the main text because it is more robust against seagull artifacts: it finds an origin that is consistent across several beads without the need to estimate additional experimental parameters such as \(r\), \(y_0\), or \(\psi\).
