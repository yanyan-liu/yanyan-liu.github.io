Background¶

Proposed in 2017, Maximally INFormative Luminescence eXcitation (MINFLUX) is an emergent localization strategy that reduces the number of photons required to achieve a given localization precision in comparison to camera-based approaches [32] (2017). It relies on utilizing tailored excitation beam patterns sequentially to probe the location of a fluorophore. MINFLUX achieves single-digit nanometer isotropic localization precision in three dimensions of fixed and living cells [198] and sub-millisecond time resolution for single-molecule tracking [199]. Using minimal number of photons, MINFLUX reduces photobleaching and enables long observation time, which makes it a suitable technique to study cellular dynamics at the molecular level [30, 200].

Principle of MINFLUX¶

The general principle of MINFLUX is as follows. The excitation light goes through a beam shaper such as a spatial light modulator (SLM) that modifies the pupil phase such that the resulting beam pattern has a zero of intensity at the focal plane where the sample is placed (Fig. 42 (a) and (b)).

Such an isotropic shape has a characteristic low intensity at its center, thus termed the zero of the beam. Measurements in MINFLUX correspond to the fluorescence photon counts for different excitation beams, collected by an avalanche photodiode (APD). The counts are then used to infer the spatial location of the fluorescent target. In the original MINFLUX experiment [32], four donut beams are used and arranged into a specific spatial layout: one at the center of the object plane and the other three on a circle of diameter \(L\) (Fig. 42 (b)) to obtain sufficient information for the inference step. The smaller \(L\) is, the better the localization precision and the smaller the field of view (FOV) where the precision is high.

Motivation and Contribution¶

Two beam shapes, the donut [32] and half-moon beams [30], have been proposed for MINFLUX experiments, yet their optimality remains an open question. In this section, we deploy a framework based on PSF engineering to determine optimal excitation patterns for MINFLUX. Our framework is a pipeline that takes the coefficients of the Zernike polynomials as input and outputs a loss value of the corresponding experimental setup. It is composed of sequential modules that model the synthesis of the pupil mask, the light propagation, MINFLUX detection, the statistical quantification of the localization precision, and the definition of the loss function. The framework is efficiently implemented in PyTorch to leverage the automatic differentiation functionality and powerful optimization algorithms. We show that the conventional donut beam is a robust optimum when the excitation beams are all constrained to the same shape. Further, our framework yields two pairs of half-moon beams (orthogonal to each other) which can improve the theoretical localization precision by a factor of about two.
