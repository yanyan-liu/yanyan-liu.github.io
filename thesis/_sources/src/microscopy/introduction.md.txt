(ms-intro)=
# Introduction

In the previous two chapters, we have focused on two imaging techniques that aim to achieve a large penetration
depth beyond the diffusion limit.
In the last contribution of the thesis, we switch to resolution, the other important aspect of imaging that has been
introduced in section [](#intro-imaging-resolution) in chapter [](#intro-biomedical-optical-imaging).

We recall that the spatial resolution of optical imaging techniques is governed by the optical diffraction limit,
which is defined by the numerical aperture (NA) and the excitation wavelength {math}`\lambda`.
For instance, a confocal microscope can achieve a resolution of ~200 nm given a NA of 1.4 and
{math}`\lambda=560` nm.
While this is adequate to study cells and organelles, it is not sufficient to observe proteins in their
cellular environments.
Over the past two decades, advances in optical microscopy have pushed resolution beyond the diffraction limit,
achieving spatial precision down to tens of nanometers.
Stimulated emission depletion (STED) microscopy was among the first techniques to achieve practical
super-resolution imaging of subcellular structures {cite:p}`hell1994breaking`.
These methods now allow researchers to resolve individual proteins within living cells,
which offers insight into molecular dynamics underlying cellular function and pathology
{cite:p}`Scarselli2016, wolff_minflux_2023`.

In this chapter, we will focus on localization microscopy, a type of super-resolution microscopy that is
specialized in localization and tracking of single molecules.
In particular, we are interested in the point spread function (PSF) of the imaging system, a key enabling feature
that is central to localization microscopy.

## Overview

(ms-intro-overview)=
### Localization Microscopy

As mentioned in [](#intro-imaging-beyond-the-diffraction-limit),
fluorescent super-resolution microscopy techniques break the diffraction limit of light to achieve resolutions
as good as a few nanometers by manipulating the emission state of fluorophores {cite:p}`hell_far-field_2007`.
Typical examples include STED (1994) {cite:p}`hell1994breaking`,
STORM (2006) {cite:p}`rust_sub-diffraction-limit_2006`,
PALM (2006) {cite:p}`betzig_imaging_2006`,
and a recent emerging technique called MINFLUX
(2017) {cite:p}`balzarotti_nanometer_2017`.
By virtue of localizing single molecules, they are widely used in the study of the structure and function of proteins
in life sciences {cite:p}`Sillibourne2011, Scarselli2016, Veeraraghavan2016, masch2018, vavrdova_multicolour_2019`.
In the first part of this chapter, we focus on MINFLUX
(details on this technique are provided in section [](#minflux-background) of [](#chapter-minflux)).

An important aspect of localization microscopy is its precision.
With the help of information theory, in particular, the Cramér-Rao bound (CRB),
one is able to quantify the precision of localization microscopy
{cite:p}`shechtman_optimal_2014, tzang_two-photon_2019, nehme_deepstorm3d_2020,
dong2021fundamental, Fu2022, opatovski_depth-enhanced_2024`.
Information theory shows that the variance of any unbiased estimator for the spatial coordinates of the single
molecule to be localized is larger than the inverse of the Fisher information matrix (i.e. the CRB).
Since it is independent of the estimator, CRB provides a theoretical lower limit on the variance of the
estimation, and thus the attainable precision (square root of the CRB) {cite:p}`ober_localization_2004`.
We will detail this part in [](#chapter-minflux).

(ms-intro-psf)=
### Point Spread Function

The key to localization techniques such as STED and MINFLUX is the engineering of their point spread function (PSF).
Also referred to as the impulse response, PSF is a fundamental concept that encapsulates key features of
an optical microscope.
In particular, the excitation PSF describes the shape of light that excites the fluorophores, which is determined
by the illumination laser and the objective lens;
the detection PSF represents the shape of light on the camera, which depends on the paths of the emitted photons
and is usually influenced by the objective, the emission filter, and the pinhole (when it is used, e.g. in confocal
microscopy).
Through the design of novel PSFs and excitation patterns, one can achieve a spatial resolution beyond
the Abbe limit, as has been discussed in [](#intro-imaging-diffraction).

PSF engineering has been a well studied topic in the field of light microscopy.
For instance, in camera-based 3D localization, work has been done in engineering the excitation
and/or detection PSFs
{cite:p}`shechtman_optimal_2014, roider_3d_2017, tzang_two-photon_2019, nehme_deepstorm3d_2020, Fu2022,
opatovski_depth-enhanced_2024`.
A common strategy of PSF engineering is via its corresponding phase mask (in Fourier space) expressed on a set of
basis functions, e.g. Zernike polynomials {cite:p}`lakshminarayanan_zernike_2011`.
Then, the optimization of the shape of the PSF is indirectly achieved by optimizing the phase mask instead,
over a loss metric most often based on the CRB.
Such an approach has been applied to 3D localization in a wide field configuration
{cite:p}`shechtman_optimal_2014`.

(ms-intro-contributions)=
## Motivation and Contribution

The focus of this chapter, which divided in two parts, is design and precise characterization of PSFs
for localization microscopy.

In the first part, we investigate how to improve the localization precision of MINFLUX via PSF engineering.
We develop an optimization pipeline using CRB as a performance metric to investigate optimal MINFLUX
excitation configurations through simulation.
This computational framework is a fast and low-cost alternative to experimental explorations,
and it provides quick insights on possible directions for discovering new beam shapes.
The pipeline has a flexible modular structure: it takes as input the coefficients of the Zernike polynomials that
parameterize the pupil function, then models light propagation,
and finally outputs the average CRB over a defined region of interest.
Such a forward model is nonlinear and the optimization of the pupil function is nonconvex.
As we have discussed in [](#intro-computational-aspects) under the general framework of
[](#intro-modeling-and-solving-inverse-problems),
we tackle these challenges via the automatic differentiation capability of PyTorch, a popular
deep-learning library, to solve the optimization problem.
We first confirm that the donut beam is an optimal excitation PSF under the constraint
of a single excitation beam shape;
we then show that two pairs of orthogonal half-moon beams lead to a further increase
in the localization precision by a factor of two if more than one beam shape is
possible, which has recently been implemented experimentally
{cite:p}`geismann2023fast, deguchi2024simple`.
Finally, we complement the numerical results with a mathematical analysis of the optimality based on the underlying
physical intuition: steepness of the gradient of electric field.

In the second part of this chapter, we shift our focus from the specific light propagation model
used in MINFLUX to the precise characterization of PSFs in the broader context of localization microscopy.
Particularly for high-NA systems, the scalar Fourier model is insufficient to account for the polarization of the
electric field and the non-paraxial angles.
Accurate computation of PSFs thus relies on a more precise model of the propagation of the electric field
as well as correction factors to compensate for aberrations that may occur in the microscope.
For instance, the Gibson-Lanni factor is responsible for handling spherical aberrations caused by the mismatch
of refractive indices between different layers, e.g. the sample and the immersion medium
{cite:p}`Gibson:92`.
While numerous studies have covered modeling and computing PSFs, a unified framework that systematically relates
these approaches and compares their formalisms and parameterizations is missing.
Therefore, we propose a unifying framework for PSF models in which we show the equivalence
between the two common approaches, one via Fourier transforms in the Cartesian coordinates,
and the other via Bessel functions on spherical coordinates.
In addition to the unifying theory, we distribute an open-source PyTorch-based library called _PSF Generator_.
It inherits all functionalities of PyTorch, including automatic differentiation,
which enables seamless integration with deep learning workflows.
We benchmark the two classes of models on CPU and GPU and show that
the Bessel model is advantageous for axisymmetric PSFs, while the Fourier one is more general and
thus more suitable for applications such as PSF engineering.
