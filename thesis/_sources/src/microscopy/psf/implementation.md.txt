## Design and Usage

We provide a high-performance open-source PyTorch-based library _PSF Generator_ {cite:p}`psf-library`
to generate 2D and 3D PSFs.
The library implements the four PSF models described in the previous section:

- `ScalarCartesianPropagator` ({eq}`eq:cartesian-scalar`);
- `ScalarSphericalPropagator` ({eq}`eq:polar-scalar`);
- `VectorialCartesianPropagator` ({eq}`eq:cartesian-vectorial`);
- `VectorialSphericalPropagator` ({eq}`eq:polar-vectorial-final`).

Users can choose between these propagator types, as well as configure physical (e.g., numerical aperture,
wavelength, and field of view), and numerical parameters (e.g., image dimensions and number of z-planes).
Our library also allows users to freely apply any kind of correction factors tailored
to their microscope on any chosen propagator as described in [](#psf-arbitrary-phase-aberrations).
The propagators use these parameters to first define the far field (pupil) and propagate it to obtain the focus field
(PSF).
Finally, the user can visualize, save, and export the generated PSF using our built-in utility functions.
Written in PyTorch, the library easily integrates into deep-learning workflows,
leveraging native features of PyTorch, such as automatic differentiation.

In our unifying framework, these models encompass all previously proposed PSF models based on the
Richards-Wolf integral.
As discussed in [](#psf-scalar-models), the spherical propagators require the axisymmetric assumption.
When it is valid, the Cartesian and spherical propagators perform equivalent integral computations and
result in the same PSF.
Both are proposed in our library as they differ in terms of computational efficiency and applicability.

## Code Optimization

We have designed our library to follow the convention of PyTorch to benefit from its performance optimization for
data loading and computation parallelization.
Namely, any tensor corresponding to a field has a shape of the form `(z, channel, x, y)`,
which corresponds to the typical PyTorch convention `(batch, channel, x, y)`.
Computations are performed plane-by-plane on the axial `z` axis (`batch` dimension),
while `x` and `y` correspond to the transverse sizes of 2D images.
This enables the efficient parallelization of Fourier transforms for Cartesian models and of numerical integrations
for spherical models.
Akin to grayscale versus RGB images, the `channel` dimension is equal to 1 for scalar models and
3 in the vectorial case.

The Cartesian parameterization relies on multiple calls of 2D Fourier transforms with a desired field of view
and pixel size.
To account for the very small physical dimensions associated to pixels in localization microscopy,
we have implemented a custom variant of the `2D FFT` in PyTorch.
It enables arbitrary pixel sizes and is based on the chirp Z-transform, at the cost of a convolution and three FFTs.
The computational complexity of a single plane is still {math}`O(n\log(n))` with {math}`n` the size of the
transverse plane.
This method has been introduced in previous studies of the Cartesian models
{cite:p}`leutenegger_fast_2006, miora2024calculating, liu_point_2015`, e.g.,
refer to section [](#minflux-zoomfft) in the previous chapter [](#chapter-minflux) for details.
This approach also eliminates any artifacts caused by periodic boundary conditions,
which are common with FFTs {cite:p}`miora2024calculating`.

The spherical parametrization relies on fast and accurate
evaluation of multiple one-dimensional integrals that involve the Bessel function of
first order {math}`J_0` over the interval {math}`[0, \theta_{\max}]` as defined in
{eq}`eq:polar-scalar-final` for the scalar case.
To maximize speed, we vectorize the computation of the batch of 1D integrals at
different defocus distances via _torch.vmap_.
Then, for each 1D integral, we adopt the composite Simpson's rule {cite:p}`stoer_introduction_2002`
to benefit from its high accuracy, good numerical stability, and minimal computational overhead.
The computational complexity in this case is {math}`O(n)` with {math}`n` the number of integration steps
in the interval {math}`[0, \theta_{\max}]`.
Additionally, as PyTorch does not natively support automatic differentiation of Bessel functions
as of version 2.3, we also provide a differentiable version of the Bessel functions in our library.
