# Implementation

In this section, we present the implementation of the optimization pipeline,
including

- the architecture of the forward model,
- the optimization algorithm, and
- technical details on the computation of the partial derivatives and the generalized inverse Fourier transform.

(minflux-overview-pipeline)=
## Overview of the Pipeline

The forward model described in the previous section follows a linear structure, and we implement it
as a pipeline composed of seven modules (see {numref}`fig:minflux-architecture` for an overview and
{numref}`fig:minflux-flowchart-detailed` for details).

The five core modules (Pupil, PSF, MINFLUX, Quantification, and Loss) are executed sequentially,
with the output of one as the input for the next.
The two auxiliary modules, Derivative and CRB, support Quantification.

(minflux-optimization-algorithm)=
## Optimization Algorithm

We deploy a gradient-based minimization algorithm in {prf:ref}`alg:minflux-optimization` to solve
{eq}`eq:minflux-optimization` .
We employ _Adam_, a stochastic gradient descent algorithm for solving inverse problems {cite:p}`kingma_adam_2015`.
Due to the complexity of the forward model (a sequence of common operations but overall nonlinear, as shown
in {numref}`fig:minflux-flowchart-detailed`), it is impractical to derive and implement an explicit expression
of the gradient {math}`\frac{\mathrm{d}J}{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{C}}` of the loss function.
We thus use PyTorch and leverage its automatic differentiation functionality to obtain the gradient,
which has been introduced in section [](#intro-computational-aspects)
in chapter [](#intro-modeling-and-solving-inverse-problems).
The coefficient matrix {math}`\mathbf{C}^{N_{\textrm{iter}}}` of the final iteration is used to generate the final
optimized pupil, the PSF and the corresponding CRB map.

```{prf:algorithm} MINFLUX Optimization
:label: alg:minflux-optimization

**Input**: Initial guess of the Zernike coefficient matrix {math}`\mathbf{C}^0`,
stopping threshold {math}`\epsilon=10^{-6}`, and maximal number of iterations {math}`N_{\max}`

1. {math}`N_{\textrm{iter}} = 0`
2. Calculate the loss {math}`J(\mathbf{C}^{N_{\textrm{iter}}})` in {eq}`eq:minflux-optimization`
3. Calculate the gradient {math}`\frac{\mathrm{d}J}{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{C}} (\mathbf{C}^{N_{\textrm{iter}}})` using
{math}`J.\textrm{backward()}`
4. Apply a gradient-descent step via {math}`Adam(lr=0.05, betas=(0.9, 0.999))` 
5. If ({math}`N_{\textrm{iter}} > 0` and
{math}`|J(\mathbf{C}^{N_{\mathrm{iter}}}) - J(\mathbf{C}^{N_{\textrm{iter} - 1}})| \leq \epsilon`) or
({math}` N_{\textrm{iter}} > N_{\max})`:

    Terminate.

6. {math}`N_{\textrm{iter}}\leftarrow N_{\textrm{iter}} + 1`  

```

Our pipeline has a practical multiresolution feature.
The input of the pipeline, the Zernike coefficient matrix, does not depend on the size of the pupil or the PSF.
This allows us to accelerate optimization on a coarse scale and then produce high-resolution images afterward.
For example, we used a numerical size of {math}`(256\times256)` px and {math}`(512\times512)` px for the pupil
and PSF, respectively, during optimization, which enables us to finish in a reasonable amount of time
(see {numref}`fig:minflux-loss`) while ensuring sufficient sampling of the pupil function and the CRB map.
After optimization, we perform an extra round of the forward pipeline using the optimized coefficients to yield
images of size {math}`(2048\times2048)` px.

## Technical Notes

In this section, we explain the solutions to two computational challenges in the pipeline:
- a precise formula of the partial derivatives involved in the Quantification Module
(see {numref}`fig:minflux-flowchart-detailed` and {eq}`eq:fisher-expression`), and
- a fast and efficient technique to compute the inverse Fourier transforms that are required in the PSF Module.

(minflux-explicit-expression-of-the-fisher-information-matrix)=
### Explicit Expression of the Partial Derivatives in the Fisher Information Matrix

In [](#minflux-detection-limit), we have seen that the partial derivative
{math}`\frac{\partial \mu_i(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\partial \theta}`
(equivalently {math}`\frac{\partial h_i(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\partial \theta}` because of {eq}`eq:def-mu`)
is needed to compute the Fisher matrix {eq}`eq:fisher-expression`.
We show that an explicit expression of {math}`\frac{\partial h_i(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\partial \theta}` exists,
which enables an exact implementation of the forward model.

For notational convenience, we denote the inverse Fourier transform
{math}`g(x, y):\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{C}` of the pupil function
{math}`P(k_\text{x}, k_\text{y})` as
```{math}
    g(x, y) = \mathcal{F}^{-1}\left\{P(k_\text{x}, k_\text{y})\right\}(x, y).
```
As {math}`h(x, y)` is defined as the square modulus of {math}`g`, it can be equivalently written as the element-wise
multiplication of {math}`g` with its complex conjugate {math}`g^*`
```{math}
    :label: eq:def-of-h
    
    h(x, y) = |g(x, y)|^2 = g(x, y)g^*(x, y).
```
Without loss of generality, we derive the partial derivative of {math}`h_i` with respect to {math}`x` at location
{math}`(x - t_\text{x}, y - t_\text{y})` and write that
```{math}
    :label: eq:h-derivative
    
    \frac{\partial h}{\partial x}(x - t_\text{x}, y - t_\text{y}) 
    &= \frac{\partial (g(x - t_\text{x}, y - t_\text{y}) g^*(x - t_\text{x}, y - t_\text{y}) )}{\partial x}\\
    &= \frac{\partial g}{\partial x}(x - t_\text{x}, y - t_\text{y}) g^*(x - t_\text{x}, y - t_\text{y}) 
        + \frac{\partial g^*}{\partial x}(x - t_\text{x}, y - t_\text{y}) g(x - t_\text{x}, y - t_\text{y})\\
    &= 2 \cdot \Re{\left( \frac{\partial g}{\partial x}(x - t_\text{x}, y - t_\text{y}) 
        g^*(x - t_\text{x}, y - t_\text{y})\right)}.
```
The last equality in {eq}`eq:h-derivative` is because {math}`(\frac{\partial g}{\partial x}\cdot g^*)` and
{math}`(\frac{\partial g^*}{\partial x}\cdot g)` are complex conjugate pairs.
Due to the shift property of the Fourier transform, we have that
```{math}
    :label: eq:p-bar-shifted
    
    g^*(x - t_\text{x}, y - t_\text{y}) = 
    \left(\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left\{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}(k_\text{x} t_\text{x} 
    + k_\text{y} t_\text{y})} P(k_\text{x}, k_\text{y})\right\}(x, y)\right)^*.
```
Combining the differentiation and shift property of the Fourier transform, we have that
```{math}
    :label: eq:dpdx
    
    \frac{\partial g}{\partial x}(x - t_\text{x}, y - t_\text{y}) 
    &= \mathcal{F}^{-1} \left\{ (\mathrm{j} k_\text{x})\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j} 
        (k_\text{x} t_\text{x} + k_\text{y} t_\text{y}) } \mathcal{F} 
        \left\{ g \right\} (k_\text{x}, k_\text{y}) \right\}(x, y)\\
    &= \mathcal{F}^{-1} \left\{ (\mathrm{j} k_\text{x})\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j} 
        (k_\text{x} t_\text{x} + k_\text{y} t_\text{y})} P(k_\text{x}, k_\text{y}) \right\}(x, y).
```
Combining {eq}`eq:dpdx` and {eq}`eq:p-bar-shifted` together with {eq}`eq:h-derivative`, we write the
explicit expression for {math}`\frac{\partial h}{\partial x}(x - t_\text{x}, y - t_\text{y})` as
```{math}
    :label: eq:dhdx
    \begin{aligned}
    & \frac{\partial h}{\partial x}(x - t_\text{x}, y - t_\text{y}) = \\
    & 2\cdot\Re {\left(
    \mathcal{F}^{-1} \left\{ (\mathrm{j} k_\text{x})\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j} (k_\text{x} t_\text{x} 
    + k_\text{y} t_\text{y})} P(k_\text{x}, k_\text{y}) \right\} 
    \left(\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left\{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}(k_\text{x} t_\text{x} 
    + k_\text{y} t_\text{y})} P(k_\text{x}, k_\text{y})\right\} (x, y)\right)^*
    \right)}.
    \end{aligned}
```
Similarly, the derivative {math}`\frac{\partial h}{\partial y}(x - t_\text{x}, y - t_\text{y})` of {math}`h`
with respect to {math}`y` at location {math}`(x - t_\text{x}, y - t_\text{y})` is
```{math}
    :label: eq:dhdy
    \begin{aligned}
    & \frac{\partial h}{\partial y}(x - t_\text{x}, y - t_\text{y}) = \\
    & 2\cdot\Re{ \left(
    \mathcal{F}^{-1} \left\{ (\mathrm{j} k_\text{y})\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j} 
    (k_\text{x} t_\text{x} + k_\text{y} t_\text{y})} P(k_\text{x}, k_\text{y}) \right\} 
    \left(\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left\{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}(k_\text{x} t_\text{x} 
    + k_\text{y} t_\text{y})} P(k_\text{x}, k_\text{y})\right\} (x, y)\right)^*
    \right)}.
    \end{aligned}
```

(minflux-zoomfft)=
### Efficient Computation of the Generalized Inverse Fourier Transform

We see from {eq}`eq:def-of-PSF`, {eq}`eq:dhdx`, and {eq}`eq:dhdy`
that the inverse Fourier transform (IFT) is frequently required to compute the beam-propagation model and
the partial derivatives in the Fisher information matrix.
While the standard inverse discrete Fourier transform (IDFT) algorithm is a common choice,
it is not suitable for our specific task.
To produce a high-resolution PSF image, one needs to pad many zeros, which is computationally inefficient.
Besides, IDFT can only handle the case where the size of the input and output (e.g. the pupil and the PSF)
is the same.
In this section, we address these challenges via the chirp Z-transform (CZT) and
provide an efficient algorithm to compute the generalized inverse Fourier transform.
It is of the same complexity as FFT and allows us to zoom in onto the object plane to refine the PSF function
without zero-padding.
This approach is applicable to other beam propagation models based on Fourier transforms
(see next section [](#chapter-psf) for details).

The chirp Z-transform of a discrete 1D signal {math}`x_n` of length {math}`N` is defined as
```{math}
    :label: eq:z-transform
    y_m = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x_n a^{-n} w^{nm}, \quad m=0,\ldots, M-1,
```
where {math}`y_m` is the {math}`m`th sample of the output signal of length {math}`M`,
with {math}`M` being not necessarily equal to {math}`N`.
We observe that IDFT is a special case of {eq}`eq:z-transform` with {math}`a=1`,
{math}`w=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\mathrm{j}}{N}}`, and a scaling factor of {math}`\frac{1}{N}`.
Graphically, the sampling of {math}`y_m` starts from {math}`(1, 0)` and covers the whole unit circle with a constant
angular step size of {math}`\frac{2\pi}{N}` on the complex plane, see
{numref}`fig:fft-sampling` (a).

```{figure} images/minflux_sampling.*
:name: fig:fft-sampling
:width: 75%

Sampling of {math}`N` points on the unit circle. 
(a) IDFT samples uniformly the whole unit circle. 
(b) zoomIDFT performs a uniform sampling of a subset of the unit circle.
```

We define a special IDFT, termed zoomIDFT
```{math}
    :label: eq:zoomifft
    
    y^z_m = \textrm{zoomIDFT}(x_n) :=  \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x_n \mathrm{e}^{\frac{\mathrm{j}}{N} 
        z (n - \frac{N}{2}) (m-\frac{M}{2})}, \quad m=0,\ldots, M-1,
```
where {math}`z\in(0, 1]` is a zoom factor such that only {math}`z`-percent of the full unit circle is sampled.
Further, the origin of both the input and output signals is centered instead of at the beginning as indicated by
{math}`(n - \frac{N}{2})` and {math}`(k-\frac{M}{2})`, see {numref}`fig:fft-sampling` (b).

Given the desired number of sampling points for both the Fourier and object planes, zoomIDFT allows us to control the
area on which the pupil occupies via a constant --- the zooming factor {math}`z`.
Ideally, {math}`z` is chosen such that the pupil fully occupies the whole plane for maximal numerical
efficiency (see ({numref}`fig:zooming`)).

```{figure} images/minflux_zooming_effect.*
:name: fig:zooming

Impact of the zooming factor. 
First row: Pupil functions with an insufficient (a) zooming factor of 0.00875, medium (b) factor of 0.004375,
and perfect (c) zooming of 0.0021875.
Second row: Corresponding PSFs.
The numerical sizes of the pupils and PSFs are {math}`(1028\times1028)` px and {math}`(2048\times2048)` px,
respectively.
```

To efficiently compute {eq}`eq:zoomifft`, we follow the techniques proposed in {cite:p}`bluestein_linear_1970,
rabiner_chirp_1969, leutenegger_fast_2006` and rewrite {eq}`eq:z-transform` as the convolution

```{math}
    :label: eq:conv
    
    y_m &= w^{\frac{m^2}{2}}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\left(x_n a^{-n} w^{\frac{n^2}{2}} \cdot w^{-\frac{(m-n)^2}{2}}\right)\\
        &= w^{\frac{m^2}{2}} \left((x_n a^{-n} w^{\frac{n^2}{2}}) * w^{-\frac{n^2}{2}}\right) \\
        &= w^{\frac{m^2}{2}} \cdot \textrm{IFFT}\left( \textrm{FFT}(x_n a^{-n} w^{\frac{n^2}{2}}) 
            \cdot \textrm{FFT}(w^{-\frac{n^2}{2}})\right)

```

using the relation {math}`nm = (n^2 + m^2 - (m-n)^2) / 2` {cite:p}`bluestein_linear_1970` to separate the
variables {math}`n` and {math}`m`.
{eq}`eq:conv` is then efficiently computed using two precomputed FFTs and one IFFT.
We thus shuffle the order of the terms of zoomIFFT in {eq}`eq:zoomifft` to obtain that

```{math}
    :label: eq:zoomifft-compute
    
    y^z_m = \mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\mathrm{j}}{2} z(m-\frac{M}{2})} \cdot 
        \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x_n \mathrm{e}^{(\frac{\mathrm{j}}{2} z\frac{M}{N})(-n)} 
        \mathrm{e}^{(\frac{\mathrm{j}}{N}z) (nm)}, 
```

where {math}`a = \mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j} z\frac{M}{N}}`, {math}`w=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\mathrm{j}}{N}z}`.
Then, we use {eq}`eq:conv` to compute the CZT contained in {eq}`eq:zoomifft-compute`.
