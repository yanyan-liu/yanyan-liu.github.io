(minflux-background)=
# Background

Proposed in 2017, Maximally INFormative Luminescence eXcitation (MINFLUX) is an emergent localization strategy
that reduces the number of photons required to achieve a given localization precision
in comparison to camera-based approaches {cite:p}`balzarotti_nanometer_2017` (2017).
It relies on utilizing tailored excitation beam patterns sequentially to probe the location of a fluorophore.
MINFLUX achieves single-digit nanometer isotropic localization precision in three dimensions of fixed and living
cells {cite:p}`gwosch_minflux_2020` and sub-millisecond time resolution for single-molecule tracking
{cite:p}`eilers_minflux_2018`.
Using minimal number of photons, MINFLUX reduces photobleaching and enables long observation time, which makes it a
suitable technique to study cellular dynamics at the molecular level {cite:p}`wolff_minflux_2023, carsten_minflux_2023`.

(minflux-principle)=
## Principle of MINFLUX

The general principle of MINFLUX is as follows.
The excitation light goes through a beam shaper such as a spatial light modulator (SLM) that modifies the pupil phase
 such that the resulting beam pattern has a zero of intensity at the focal plane where the sample is placed
 ({numref}`fig:minflux-donut-baseline` (a) and (b)).

```{figure} images/minflux_setup.*
SLM: spatial light modulator.

(b) Intensity distribution of a donut beam on the {math}`xy`-plane.

Three of these dots lie on a circle with diameter {math}`L=100` nm (dashed yellow circle).
The dotted red circle with radius {math}`\tau=28` nm represents the boundary of the FOV {math}`\Gamma_\tau` of
optimization.
(c) The CRB map (in nm) in the FOV {math}`\Gamma_\tau` of a standard MINFLUX experiment with beam setting as in (b)
for 100 photons.
The labels on the horizontal and vertical axes describe the relative distance in nanometers to the center of the FOV
for the CRB maps.
```

Such an isotropic shape has a characteristic low intensity at its center, thus termed the _zero_ of the beam.
Measurements in MINFLUX correspond to the fluorescence photon counts for different excitation beams,
collected by an avalanche photodiode (APD).
The counts are then used to infer the spatial location of the fluorescent target.
In the original MINFLUX experiment {cite:p}`balzarotti_nanometer_2017`, four donut beams are used and arranged into
 a specific spatial layout: one at the center of the object plane and the other three on a circle of diameter {math}`L`
 ({numref}`fig:minflux-donut-baseline` (b)) to obtain sufficient information for the inference step.
The smaller {math}`L` is, the better the localization precision and the smaller the field of view (FOV) where the
precision is high.

## Motivation and Contribution

Two beam shapes, the donut {cite:p}`balzarotti_nanometer_2017` and half-moon beams {cite:p}`wolff_minflux_2023`,
have been proposed for MINFLUX experiments,
yet their optimality remains an open question.
In this section, we deploy a framework based on PSF engineering to determine optimal excitation patterns for MINFLUX.
Our framework is a pipeline that takes the coefficients of the Zernike polynomials as input and outputs a loss value
of the corresponding experimental setup.
It is composed of sequential modules that model the synthesis of the pupil mask, the light propagation,
MINFLUX detection, the statistical quantification of the localization precision,
and the definition of the loss function.
The framework is efficiently implemented in PyTorch to leverage the automatic differentiation functionality
and powerful optimization algorithms.
We show that the conventional donut beam is a robust optimum when the excitation beams are all constrained to the same
shape.
Further, our framework yields two pairs of half-moon beams (orthogonal to each other) which can
improve the theoretical localization precision by a factor of about two.
