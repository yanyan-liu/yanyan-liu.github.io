(minflux-results)=
# Results

We present the results of two distinct groups of experiments
aimed at optimizing beam shapes for MINFLUX.
In the first group, we investigate the case in which all beams are translated
versions of each other, questioning the optimality of the well-known donut beam.
In the second one, we relax the optimization conditions to multiple beam shapes as
it has been proposed in recent experiments {cite:p}`wolff_minflux_2023, deguchi2024simple`.

Despite the inherent complexity of nonlinear model and the nonconvex optimization, the final
beam patterns we present demonstrate remarkable robustness,
consistently emerging across a wide range of initial conditions
and optimization parameters.
Following these numerical results,
we provide a mathematical analysis that substantiates these
beam shapes as optimal in a mathematical sense.

(minflux-numerical-results)=
## Numerical Optimality

(minflux-donut-numerical-results)=
### Donut Beam

In the first group, we adopt the original MINFLUX arrangement illustrated in {numref}`fig:minflux-donut-baseline` (b),
expanded to a total of seven beams, i.e. one at the center of the FOV and other six on a circle of diameter L nm,
to mitigate anisotropy in the CRB
(see {numref}`fig:minflux-donut-baseline` (c) and {numref}`fig:minflux-one-beam-result` (f)).
All seven beams are constrained to have the same shape.
This configuration simplifies the experimental implementation as it allows for a fixed
phase mask in Fourier space, while another optical element (e.g., a galvo mirror) simply translates
the excitation pattern.
We initialized the optimization with a perturbed donut beam (see {numref}`fig:minflux-one-beam-result` (a)).
To be precise, we use a vortex phase mask {math}`\kappa` and random initial coefficients {math}`\mathbf{C}`.

The results of this optimization, shown in {numref}`fig:minflux-one-beam-result` (b) and (d) exhibit a clear evolution
towards a donut shape.
{numref}`fig:minflux-one-beam-result` (g) displays the CRB line profiles and reveals a significant decrease

{numref}`fig:minflux-one-beam-result` (g).

(minflux-halfmoon-beam-numerical-results)=
### Half-Moon Beam

In the second group of experiments, we introduced a configuration of two distinct pairs of beams,
and within each pair the beams are identical.
We arranged the centers of these beams on a circle of diameter {math}`L=100`nm at locations
{math}`(\frac{L}{2}, 0), (0, \frac{L}{2}), (-\frac{L}{2}, 0)` and {math}`(0, -\frac{L}{2})` in
{math}`x-y` coordinates.
Such as a layout is termed as a _diamond_ beam setting.
We initialize with four perfect donut beams (see {numref}`fig:minflux-donut-baseline` (b))
as it is optimal for the standard setting.
In {numref}`fig:minflux-two-pairs-beam` (a), we show the image of the superposition of the intensity of
all the four donut beams and the diamond setting.
```{figure} images/minflux_two_pairs_result.*
:name: fig:minflux-two-pairs-beam
:width: 100%

(a), (c), and (e): Beams on the horizontal axis for the initial (a), optimized (c), and theoretical (e) cases.
(b), (d), and (f): Beams on the vertical axis for the initial (b), optimized (d), and theoretical (f) cases.
(g)-(j): Phase masks for the corresponding beams on the horizontal and vertical axes for the optimized and
theoretical cases.
(k)-(m): CRB maps of the initial, optimized, and theoretical cases.
(n): Horizontal line profiles through the center of the FOV of the CRB maps (k)-(m).
```

The PSF shapes evolved significantly during optimization and converged to a configuration characterized by
two approximately symmetric peaks separated by a valley of low intensities
(see {numref}`fig:minflux-two-pairs-beam` (c) and (d)).
Further, we notice that the axes of the two pairs of beams are orthogonal to each other,
oriented along the edges of the square formed by the beam centers.
The associated phase masks of these beams are shown in {numref}`fig:minflux-two-pairs-beam` (g) and (f).
This optimized configuration significantly improves the CRB values by a factor of two
(see {numref}`fig:minflux-two-pairs-beam` (k) and (l)).

The optimal beams resemble the existing _half-moon_ beams which have been seen in {cite:p}`wolff_minflux_2023`.
In practice, these shape can be generated by a {math}`\pi`-phase-shifted half disk in the pupil plane
({numref}`fig:minflux-two-pairs-beam` (i) and (j)).
These theoretical half-moon beams yield slightly more uniform CRB maps compared to the optimized PSFs, as shown
in {numref}`fig:minflux-two-pairs-beam` (l) and (m).
A close compare of the CRB line profiles or the initial, optimized, and theoretical half-moon beams is shown in
{numref}`fig:minflux-two-pairs-beam` (n).
The CRB of both the optimized and the theoretical half-moon beams greatly improves upon the initial start.

Similar to the first group of experiments, we tested the robustness of the optimized beam across
various optimization conditions, including different number of Zernike polynomials and different level of background.
The optimization consistently produces the same beam
shape (see {numref}`fig:minflux-two-pairs-beam-additional-results`).

(minflux-computational-aspects)=
### Computational Aspects

Key optimization parameters are fixed across both experiments:

- In the PSF module, the wavelength of the excitation light is 640 nm.
- The numerical aperture of the setup is 1.42.
- The background {math}`b` in {eq}`eq:def-of-PSF` is set to be 5\% of the mean value of {math}`h` on the whole object plane.
- In the pupil module, we use the first 15 Zernike polynomials ({math}`D=15`).
- The size of the object plane is {math}`(1000\times1000)` nm.
- The total number of photons received at each location is {math}`N=100`.
- The efficiency {math}`\alpha` of the experiment is assumed to be 1.
- The beam positions are defined with {math}`L = 100` nm.
- The radius {math}`\tau` of the optimization FOV is 28 nm.
- The regularization term is computed on a ROI {math}`\Omega_\text{h}` of radius 170 nm, approximately the radius of the donut beam.
- The regularization weight {math}`\gamma` is 0.1.
- The tolerance of convergence is {math}`10^{-6}`.

The computational time and the evolution of the loss of each optimization experiment presented
are shown in {numref}`fig:minflux-loss`.

(minflux-theoretical-results)=
## Theoretical Analysis of the Optimality

To complement our optimization results, we carried out a theoretical analysis of the optimality.
We demonstrate how the donut and half-moon PSFs are optimal in a specific mathematical sense.
Precisely, the optimal PSFs maximize the norm of the gradient of the electric field at the center of
the field of view: for a single direction in the half-moon case, and averaged over all directions for the donut PSF.
The half-moon case relies on an analytic proof, while the donut case relies on numerical evidence.
These results provide a heuristic explanation for the final PSF forms;
they do not account for shifts and the estimation problem, which necessitates our Fisher-information framework.

We recall the definition of the PSF intensity {math}`h: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{C}`
```{math}
:label: eq:psf-intensity

h(\mathbf{r}) = |g(\mathbf{r})|^2, \quad \mathbf{r} = (x,y),
```
where the electric field {math}`g: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{C}` is given by
```{math}
:label: eq:electric-field

g(\mathbf{r}) = \mathcal{F}^{-1}\left\{ P(\mathbf{k})\right\}(\mathbf{r}),
\quad \mathbf{k} = (k_\text{x}, k_\text{y}).
```
The pupil function {math}`(P(\mathbf{k}): \Lambda \rightarrow \mathbb{C})` is defined on a finite support
{math}`(\Lambda \subset \mathbb{R}^2)`, given by the low-pass-filtering disk that corresponds to
the numerical aperture, with the constraint that {math}`(P)` has unit modulus over {math}`(\Lambda)` so that

```{math}
    :label: eq:pupil-constraints
    \begin{cases}
        |P(\mathbf{k})| = 1, \qquad & \mathbf{k} \in \Lambda,\\
        P(\mathbf{k}) = 0, \qquad &\text{else }.
    \end{cases}
```
We start by explaining our heuristic in 1D
MINFLUX minimizes the photon flux by precisely targeting and modulating
the emission of fluorophores near the zero region of the excitation PSF.
We thus assume in 1D that {math}`h(0) = g(0) = 0` and express the Maclaurin expansion of {math}`g` as
{math}`g(x) = g'(0) x + \mathcal{O}(x^2)`,
which implies that the approximated PSF {math}`\tilde{h}(x) = |g'(0)|^2 x^2` is a centered parabola.
We aim to maximize
the curvature coefficient {math}`|g'(0)|^2` to maximize the excitation change for a change in the particle position.
This heuristic was first introduced in the original MINFLUX paper {cite:p}`balzarotti_nanometer_2017`.

(minflux-theoretical-optimality-half-moon-beam)=
### Half-Moon Beam

To extend to 2D the 1D Maclaurin approximation, we choose a direction defined by a unit vector {math}`\mathbf{n}`.
The directional gradient {math}`D_\mathbf{n} g(\mathbf{r}) \in \mathbb{C}` of {math}`g` along a
given direction {math}`\mathbf{n}` at {math}`\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{0}` is defined as

```{math}
    :label: eq:directional-gradient
    
    D_\mathbf{n} g(\mathbf{0}) 
        = \left(\underset{t \to 0}{\text{lim}}
        \left.\frac{g(\mathbf{r} + t\mathbf{n}) - g(\mathbf{r})}{ t}\right)\right|_{\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{0}}. 
```

From {eq}`eq:pupil-constraints` and the properties of Fourier transform,
{eq}`eq:directional-gradient` can be expressed as

```{math}
    :label: eq:directional-gradient-ft
    
    D_\mathbf{n} g(\mathbf{0}) 
    &= \mathcal{F}^{-1} \left\{\mathrm{j} (\mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{n})P(\mathbf{k}) \right\}(\mathbf{0}) \\
    &= \frac{\mathrm{j}}{2\pi}\iint_\Lambda P(\mathbf{k}) (\mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{n}) \mathrm{d} \mathbf{k}. 
```   

where {math}`\cdot` denotes the scalar product between two vectors.
Our aim is to find the pupil function {math}`P` that maximizes the directional metric

```{math}
    :label: eq:directional-metric
    
    m_\mathbf{n}(P) = \left|\iint_\Lambda P(\mathbf{k}) (\mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{n}) \mathrm{d} \mathbf{k}\right|^2.
```

Using the triangular inequality and {eq}`eq:pupil-constraints`, we have that

```{math}
    :label: eq:half-moon-optimal-demonstration
    
    m_\mathbf{n}(P) &
    \leq \left(\iint_\Lambda \left| P(\mathbf{k}) (\mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{n}) \right|\mathrm{d}\mathbf{k} \right)^2 \\
    &= \left(\iint_\Lambda \left| P(\mathbf{k})\right| 
        \left| (\mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{n}) \right|\mathrm{d}\mathbf{k} \right)^2 \\
    &= \left(\iint_\Lambda  \left| (\mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{n}) \right|\mathrm{d}\mathbf{k} \right)^2.
```

Equality is achieved when {math}`P(\mathbf{k}) (\mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{n}) = \mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j} \alpha}`
for a constant {math}`\alpha` for all {math}`\mathbf{k} \in \Lambda`.
This yields that
{math}`P(\mathbf{k}) = \mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}\operatorname{Arg}(\mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{n}) + \alpha}`
and corresponds to a half-moon pupil function as depicted in {numref}`fig:projected-power-iterations-result` (b).
This half-moon pupil function is characterized by a {math}`\pi`-phase jump between the two half disks.
It yields the optimal PSF that maximizes the gradient of the electric field in a single direction.

If one wants to minimize a single-directional gradient norm, the optimal pupil function corresponds to
a half-moon PSF.
This supports the result of our CRB optimization as we obtain two PSFs that maximize gradients
along orthogonal directions to solve our 2D localization-estimation problem.

(minflux-theoretical-optimality-donut-beam)=
### Donut Beam

Another desirable metric to optimize is the isotropic version of {math}`m_\mathbf{n}`, averaging over all directions
{math}`\mathbf{n}` on the unit circle {math}`O`. This metric is

```{math}
    :label: eq:isotropic-metric
    
    m_O(P) = \int_O \left|\iint_\Lambda P(\mathbf{k}) (\mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{n}) \mathrm{d} \mathbf{k}\right|^2
    \mathrm{d} \mathbf{n}.
```

This expression is less straightforward to maximize.
Still, we can reformulate it as a quadratic-programming problem in the Hilbert space {math}`\mathcal{H}` of
compactly supported functions {math}`f: \Lambda \rightarrow \mathbb{C}` with the Hermitian product
{math}`\langle f^\mathrm{H}, g\rangle = \iint_\Lambda f^*(\mathbf{k}) g(\mathbf{k})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{k}`.
For convenience, we will denote by {math}`p \in \mathcal{H}` the pupil function and, for any unit vector
{math}`\mathbf{n}`, {math}`k_\mathbf{n} \in \mathcal{H}` as

```{math}
    :label: eq:def-k-scalar-n-hilbert
    
    k_\mathbf{n}: \mathbf{k} \rightarrow (\mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{n})^*.
```

The metric to optimize can be rewritten as

```{math}
    :label: eq:exchange-operator-place

    m_O(P) 
    &= \int_O |k_\mathbf{n}^\mathrm{H} p|^2 \mathrm{d}\mathbf{n} \\
    &= \int_O p^\mathrm{H} k_\mathbf{n} k_\mathbf{n}^\mathrm{H} p \mathrm{d}\mathbf{n} \\     
    &= p^\mathrm{H} \left(\int_O k_\mathbf{n} k_\mathbf{n}^\mathrm{H} \mathrm{d}\mathbf{n}\right) p  \\
    &= p^\mathrm{H} K p,
```

where we have introduced the symmetric linear operator
{math}`K = \int_O k_\mathbf{n} k_\mathbf{n}^\mathrm{H} \mathrm{d}\mathbf{n}`,
operating in the Hilbert space {math}`\mathcal{H}`.
To obtain {eq}`eq:exchange-operator-place`, the reordering of the integrals is legitimated by the compact support of functions in
{math}`\mathcal{H}`.

Hence, we want to maximize this quadratic form over the set of pupil functions {math}`p \in \mathcal{H}`
with the unit element-wise norm constraint of {eq}`eq:pupil-constraints`. We compute the solution numerically
using projected power iterations as

```{math}
    :label: eq:projected-power-iterations
    
    \begin{cases}
        p_0 : \mathbf{k} \rightarrow 1, \\
        p_{i+1} = \text{Proj}( K p_i ), \text{ for } i = 0, 1, \ldots.
    \end{cases}
```

with the projector on the constraint set defined as:

```{math}
    :label: eq:projector-definition
    
    \text{Proj}(f): \mathbf{k} \rightarrow \begin{cases}
        \frac{f(\mathbf{k})}{|f(\mathbf{k})|}, \quad & f(\mathbf{k}) \neq 0 \\
        1, \quad & \text{ otherwise.}
    \end{cases}
```

We see in {numref}`fig:projected-power-iterations-result` (b) the results of these power iterations.
They yield the phase ramp phase characteristics of the donut PSF.
The integral over {math}`\mathbf{n} \in O` is discretized uniformly over the unit circle with 100 terms.
The pupil function {math}`p \in \mathcal{H}` is discretized over a uniform grid for {math}`(k_\text{x}, k_\text{y})`,
as a {math}`(2048\times2048)` px image.
A total of 100 power iterations is performed.

The projected power iterations employed for our complex-valued quadratic-programming problem with quadratic
constraints lack theoretical convergence guarantees.
Similar formulations arise in other applications, such as electromagnetic beamforming {cite:p}`he_effects_2022`.
However, by repeating the power iterations with different initial guesses,
the donut pupil function consistently emerges as the solution, which demonstrates its robustness.

If one wants to minimize the directional derivative norm averaged along all directions,
the optimal pupil function corresponds to a donut PSF.
This supports the result of our CRB optimization as we obtain the donut PSF when only a single shifted PSF is allowed.
