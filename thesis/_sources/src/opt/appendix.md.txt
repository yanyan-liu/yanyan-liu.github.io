# Appendix: Cone-Beam Shift of Origin due to Off-Center Rotation

While we have shown that the RI mismatch transforms the parallel problem into a conic one, the center that yields
the optimal FDK reconstruction is not the {math}`y_0` predicted by {eq}`eq:opt-optical-origin-of-cone-beam`, but rather an {math}`y_r`
that is significantly closer to the object, with {math}`y_0>y_r`.
Following recent evidence regarding the effects of recalibration on seagull artifacts {cite:p}`Schmidt:21`, we have
established experimentally that a mis\-centering of the cylinder with respect to the axis of the turning platform is at
the origin of this offset.

To reconcile the conic geometry motivated in {eq}`eq:opt-optical-origin-of-cone-beam` with this newly characterized experimental error,
we proceed to show that the offset of the center of rotation accumulates the original projections in a way that
generates an alternative cone with its center displaced towards the origin.
Indeed, even when misaligned, the platform still rotates the sample fully, and hence, generates complete tomographic data.
The center of a cone beam corresponds to the position through which all light rays pass; we thus look for the point
where the flux of intensity is maximal.

```{figure} optical/images/off_center.*
:name: fig:opt-optical-offcenter-geometry
:width: 80%
Effect of the off-centered rotation of the sample on the center of the cone beam. The bigger black disk
corresponds to the cylinder in {numref}`fig:opt-optical-setup`, whereas the greyish inner circle represents its orbit around
the true center (size exaggerated). The rotation trajectory (in blue) is translated into an oscillation (also in blue)
of the cone's center that generates an alternative cone closer to the sample.
``` 

The effect of the rotation on the {math}`y` direction being negligible because {math}`y_0 \gg R`, we focus instead on
the movement induced in the {math}`x` direction.
This displacement can be expressed as {math}`r\cos \theta`, where {math}`\theta` is the angle of rotation and {math}`r`
he distance between the center of the sample and the true axis.
At each angle of rotation, the rays originate a light cone that is viewed from {math}`y_0` with an angle {math}`\psi`,
which in turn depends on {math}`R` ({numref}`fig:opt-optical-offcenter-geometry`).
These cones accumulate on the line segment {math}`[-r,r] \times \{y_0\}` (blue in
{numref}`fig:opt-optical-offcenter-geometry`) according to the amplitude of rotation.

Let us establish the coordinate system {math}`(x,y')` centered on {math}`(0,y_0)` and with the axis of symmetry flipped,
so that {math}`y=(y_0-y')`.
In the {math}`(x, y')` system of coordinates, the finite domain limits the spatial influence of the rays, making them
all coincide at {math}`x=0`, {math}`y'=y'^\star = r \cot \left( \psi /2 \right)`, and thus creating an equivalent cone
center under fairly general conditions.
To show this, we use {math}`f(x,y')` to model the brightness within the span of each cone and {math}`w(x)` to weight
their contributions in a way that reflects how the projections concentrate at the poles.
Together, they multiply to yield the weighted intensity flux
```{math}
g (x, y') = f \left( \sqrt{x^2+y'^2} \right) w(x).
```
All contributions can then be combined by integrating over the {math}`x` movement as
```{math}
I(y')/2 = \left\{
\begin{aligned} 
      & \int_0^r g\left( x, y' \right) \,\text{d}x,  \quad y' \geq y'^\star \\
      &\int_0^{y'\tan(\psi/2)} g\left( x, y' \right)\,\text{d}x,  \quad y'<y'^\star,
   \end{aligned}
\right.
```
which is split into two components precisely because the offset rotation has a finite amplitude.
Note that the weighted intensity energy {math}`I` is continuous as {math}`y'=y'^\star` is the solution to
{math}`r=y'\tan(\psi/2)`, and that the factor of 2 appears due to the symmetry of {math}`g` with respect to
the integration domain.

The maximum of {math}`I` is attained at {math}`y'^\star` when {math}`g` is constant, thus originating a (uniform)
cone beam closer ({math}`y'^\star>0`) to the object.
We now show that such a maximum still exists under quite general physical conditions.
On one hand, brightness is to be conserved, one expects the divergence of {math}`f` to vanish in the radial direction
{math}`\rho` of the corresponding polar reference system, yielding that
{math}`\nabla \cdot f = \rho^{-1} \partial \left( \rho f \right) / \partial \rho=0` and, thus,
that {math}`f \propto \rho^{-1} = 1/\sqrt{x^2+y'^2}`.
On the other hand, the weighting function can be thought to be proportional to the derivative of the geometric
projection of the rotation, like {math}`w\propto \left|\sin (\pi x/2r) \right|`.
Together, {math}`f` and {math}`w` combine into a {math}`g \propto \sin (\pi x/2r) / \sqrt{x^2+y'^2}` that makes
{math}`I` monotonically increasing for {math}`y'<y'^\star` and decreasing for {math}`y'>y'^\star` because the
integration domain shrinks progressively.
Therefore, with the maximum {math}`y'^\star=\text{argmax}_{y' \in [0, \infty)} I`, this showcases the formation of
an equivalent cone beam that is closer to the object. With respect to the original system of coordinates, the shifted
cone-beam center is at {math}`y_r=(y_0-y'^\star)`.

To find the center {math}`y_r` of the cone beam in practice, we resort to the automatic calibration scheme presented
in the main text because it is more robust against seagull artifacts: it finds an origin that is consistent across
several beads without the need to estimate additional experimental parameters such as {math}`r`, {math}`y_0`,
or {math}`\psi`.
