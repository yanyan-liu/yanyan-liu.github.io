(opt-optical-problem-formulation)=
# Problem Statement

Before an emission OPT experiment, the rotation axis is carefully adjusted to be perpendicular to the optical axis,
so that the projection data of one slice of the volume are recorded in a row of pixels on the detector
{cite:p}`Sharpe2004`.
When the effect of refraction, diffraction, scattering, or defocusing is negligible,
the emitted light propagates through the sample, the RI-matching liquid,
and the telecentric lens system in parallel straight lines (see {numref}`fig:opt-optical-setup`).
````{subfigure} A|B
:name: fig:opt-optical-setup
:width: 80

   ```{image} images/opt_optical_setup.*
       :alt: OPT geometry
   ```
   
   ```{image} images/opt_refraction.*
       :alt: Refraction
   ```

Emission OPT geometry in the ideal case and in the non-ideal case where the refractive indices do not match.
(Top) Emission OPT viewed from the top.
The sample, fixed in agarose gel of RI {math}`n_1`, is placed inside a glass container filled with an immersion liquid
of RI {math}`n_2`.
The fluorophores inside the sample emit light that propagates through the object-space telecentric lens system and gets
recorded by the camera.
More details about the experimental system can be found in {cite:p}`Schmidt:21`.
(Bottom) Ideal case (orange) and RI mismatch (blue).
Only rays parallel to the optical axis are considered due to the low-NA fluorescence collection in OPT.
The RI {math}`n_1`  of the agarose differs from the RI {math}`n_2` of the immersion liquid.
This creates a lensing effect, with a virtual focus at position {math}`O'` on the optical axis.
````
Accordingly, the standard forward model for OPT is the Radon transform {cite:p}`natterer_mathematics_2001, Koljonen19`,
which in 3D is typically inverted in a slice-by-slice fashion using the filtered backprojection (FBP) algorithm
{cite:p}`Sharpe2004, natterer_mathematics_2001`.
In reality, when the RI of the sample does not match the RI of the immersion liquid,
the path along which light propagates is altered so that the parallel-beam assumption no longer holds.

(opt-optical-modeling)=
# Modeling of RI Mismatch

Let us consider a 2D transverse slice of the sample in the liquid bath as depicted in
{numref}`fig:opt-optical-setup`.
We denote the RI of the agarose gel and the liquid bath as {math}`n_1` and {math}`n_2`,
respectively, and the radius of the cylinder as {math}`R`.
When {math}`n_1=n_2`, light follows parallel straight lines as it travels through the sample,
liquid bath, and finally the flat wall of the cuvette (see the orange lines in {numref}`fig:opt-optical-setup`).
When {math}`n_1 < n_2`,
light is refracted at the interface that separates the agarose cylinder from the liquid bath.
Propagation of the refracted light is illustrated by the blue lines in {numref}`fig:opt-optical-setup`.
The extended incident rays (blue lines inside the cylinder in {numref}`fig:opt-optical-setup`)
intersect at a point {math}`O'` on the optical axis (dotted red lines in {numref}`fig:opt-optical-setup`).
Hence, the front wall (close to the detector side)
of the cylinder can be thought of as a diverging lens whose virtual focus {math}`O'` is on the optical axis.
Rays inside the cylinder can then be approximated by a fan beam,
the origin of which coincides with the virtual focus.

Such a picture is based on two common assumptions in ray optics, to keep our model computationally tractable.
First, we make the paraxial assumption, namely, that the point emitters are close to the optical axis.
Second, we consider difference in RIs to be small ({math}`\Delta n = |n_2-n_1| \ll n_1`),
so that the resulting origin of the fan beam is far away from the cylinder.
Under these assumptions,
we can calculate the location of the virtual focus of the diverging lens (i.e., the origin of the fan beam)
as follows.
Let us focus on the 2D transverse slice of the cylinder shown in {numref}`fig:opt-optical-cone-beam-geometry`).

```{figure} images/opt_geometry.*
:name: fig:opt-optical-cone-beam-geometry
:width: 80%

Geometry of light refraction at the interface of the cylinder and the immersion liquid.
RIs inside and outside the circle are {math}`n_1` and {math}`n_2`, respectively.
```

Relevant quantities are:

- {math}`R`: radius of the disk
- {math}`A (x_A, y_a)`: position of emitter
- {math}`P (\hat x, \hat y)`: incident point on the surface of the cylinder
- {math}`\delta x>0`: vertical displacement between the emitter and {math}`P`
- {math}`O'`: intersection of the incident ray with {math}`y`-axis
- {math}`\theta`: refracted angle such that the refracted ray is parallel to the optical axis
- {math}`(\theta + \delta \theta)`: incident angle
- {math}`\Delta n=(n_2-n_1)`: RI mismatch which is assumed to be small with respect to {math}`n_1`

Snell's law states that

```{math}
:label: eq:snell

    n_2\sin\theta=n_1\sin(\theta+\delta\theta)\approx n_1(\sin\theta+\delta\theta\cos\theta),
```

where we performed a first-order Taylor expansion.
{eq}`eq:snell` implies that

```{math}
:label: eq:deltatheta

    \delta\theta=\frac{n_2-n_1}{n_1}\tan\theta.
```

Because {math}`\delta\theta\ll1`, in the right triangle with the longest side AP, one has that
```{math}
:label: eq:deltax

    \delta\theta\approx\tan\delta\theta=\frac{\delta x}{\hat y-y_A},
```
with {math}`\hat y = \sqrt{R^2-(x_A-\delta x)^2} = \sqrt{R^2 - x_A^2} + \mathcal{O}(\Delta n)`, 
noticing {math}`\mathcal{O}(\delta x) \sim \mathcal{O}(\Delta n)`.
Combining {eq}`eq:deltax` and {eq}`eq:deltatheta`, we obtain that

```{math}
:label: eq:formula1

    \delta x =\left(\hat y-y_A\right)\frac{\Delta n}{n_1}\tan\theta.
```

We also have that

```{math}
:label: eq:tantheta

    \tan \theta = -\frac{x_A + \delta x}{\hat y}.
```

Substitution of {eq}`eq:tantheta` into {eq}`eq:formula1` at order 1 in {math}`\Delta n` yields that

```{math}
:label: eq:formula2

    \delta x=\left(y_A - \hat y \right)\frac{\Delta n x_A}{n_1\hat y}.
```

The line (AP) thus satisfies that
```{math}
    y - y_A = \frac{\hat y - y_A}{\delta x} (x - x_A)  
    = -\frac{n_1 \hat y}{\Delta n x_A} (x - x_A).
```

The intersection for which {math}`x=0` corresponds to

```{math}
    y_0 = y_A + \frac{n_1 \sqrt{R^2-x_A^2}}{\Delta n}.
```

For {math}`x_A \ll R` (i.e., for points close to the optical axis, which corresponds to the paraxial approximation),
we have

```{math}
    y_0 = y_A + \frac{n_1 R}{\Delta n}.
```

The first term {math}`y_A` is negligible compared to the second term as {math}`\Delta n \ll 1` and {math}`y_A\leqslant R`.
This implies that the distance of the origin {math}`O'` of the fan beam to the origin of the coordinate system
{math}`O` is much larger than the sample diameter.
We thus obtain that
```{math}
    :label: eq:opt-optical-origin-of-cone-beam
    
    y_0=\frac{n_1}{n_2-n_1}R.
```
The location {math}`y_0` of the origin {math}`O'` depends on the radius
{math}`R` of the cylinder and the refractive indices ({math}`n_1,n_2`), but not
on the position of our point {math}`A` in the paraxial approximation.
Further, the unitless quantity {math}`y_0/R` is not affected by the magnification of the imaging system.
We thus obtain a fan-beam geometry where all the rays inside the cylinder intersect at {math}`O'`.
When {math}`n_1=n_2`, the origin of the fan beam becomes {math}`+\infty`;
hence, the cone reduces to the standard parallel-beam model, as expected.

# Correction Method

We see that when {math}`n_1 \neq n_2`,
the physical model in {eq}`eq:opt-optical-origin-of-cone-beam` differs from the one typically used for reconstruction
with a parallel-beam geometry.
Yet, the proposed virtual-lens model is a better approximation of the actual physics.
We are going to show that
it is capable of reproducing and eventually correcting for some of the reconstruction artifacts.
This 2D analysis corresponds to a fan-beam model;
we can extend it to a cone-beam model when the height of the volume is sufficiently small.
Cone-beam geometries are common in other tomographic applications,
which allows us to leverage efficient implementations and reconstruction algorithms.

While {eq}`eq:opt-optical-origin-of-cone-beam` provides a good approximation of the beam origin in an idealized setting,
two additional challenges arise in practice.
First, the RI of the agarose gel evolves to match that of the immersion liquid (e.g., BABB) due to molecular diffusion.
This process may extend over the standard clearing protocol time, making it difficult to measure the value of {math}`n_1`
and thus to calculate the origin using {eq}`eq:opt-optical-origin-of-cone-beam`.
Second, there is another physical effect to consider: the cylinder is displaced as it rotates because of an experimental
offset between the axis of rotation and axis of the cylinder.
Despite their best effort, instrumentalists have not yet been able to calibrate their setups
accurately enough to avoid this offset {cite:p}`Koljonen19, Walls:07, Koskela19`.
For example, the seagull artifacts in {cite:p}`Schmidt:21` (cf. Fig. 2f therein) can be reduced
by decreasing the offset between the axis of the cylinder and the axis of rotation.
In the [](#opt-optical-cone-beam-shift-of-origin-due-to-off-center-rotation),
we show that the cone-beam geometry remains a good approximation under these conditions,
but with an origin that is closer to the sample than {eq}`eq:opt-optical-origin-of-cone-beam`.

To tackle both challenges at once,
we propose a method to automatically determine the optimal origin of the cone beam by minimizing a heuristic loss metric.
More details are provided in the [](#opt-optical-results) section.
