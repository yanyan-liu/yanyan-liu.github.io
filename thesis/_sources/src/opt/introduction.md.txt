# Introduction

For many applications, the framework of a precise physical model combined with efficient reconstruction algorithms has
proven effective, as demonstrated in Fourier ptychography {cite:p}`ou_embedded_2014`
and cryo-electron microscopy {cite:p}`zehni_joint_2020`.
In this chapter, we focus on optical projection tomography (OPT), a simple yet powerful pure optical technique
that pushes the boundaries of the optical diffusion limit to achieve deep-tissue imaging.
We show how the systematic approach introduced in [](#intro-modeling-and-solving-inverse-problems)
enables us to correct mechanical and optical artifacts due to imprecision and mismatches in the imaging system.

## Overview of OPT

Conventional optical microscopy, such as confocal microscopy, is limited to imaging relatively thin samples.
This constraint can be partially overcome using OPT, proposed by Sharpe in 2002 {cite:p}`Sharpe2004`.
OPT functions as an optical analog to X-ray CT (enabled by tissue optical clearing),
and bridges the imaging gap between MRI and confocal microscopy.
It achieves cellular-level resolution with molecular specificity over a large field-of-view (FOV)
{cite:p}`Sharpe2004, Walls2005`.
Over the years, OPT has become a mature tool for the production of high-resolution 3D
images of biological samples at mesoscopic scale {cite:p}`Lindsey2017 ,Schmidt:21` in
a brightfield {cite:p}`Wang:07, bassi:11` or fluorescence
{cite:p}`Walls2007, Walls2005, McGinty:11, Torres2021` configuration.
OPT is widely used in a variety of applications, such as the mapping of the distribution of proteins in embryos
{cite:p}`Sharpe2002, Sharpe2004`,
the localization of metastases in lymph nodes {cite:p}`Torres2021`,
the display of vascular networks and amyloid depositions in the mouse brain model to study Alzheimer's disease
{cite:p}`Lindsey2017`,
and the imaging of the spatial arrangement of intestinal villi {cite:p}`Schmidt:21`.

OPT operates in two modes: emission and transmission.
In emission OPT, widefield illumination excites the fluorophores in the sample; the probe is
the emitted optical photons and the light-matter interaction is fluorescence.
In transmission OPT, diffuse white light passes through the sample, and
the measurements reflect attenuation of the incident light {cite:p}`Walls2005`.
As a tomographic imaging technology, OPT falls into the same category as X-ray CT and SPECT.

In an OPT experiment, the sample undergoes a clearing process before being imaged, which renders it transparent to
reduce scattering and heterogeneities of refractive index {cite:p}`Sharpe2002`.
This allows photons to traverse a thick sample up to several millimeters.
The sample is then embedded in agarose gel and immersed in a refractive-index-matched liquid (most often BABB).
A motorized stage holds the sample and rotates it to a series of angular positions.
A lens system focuses light on the focal plane,
and a CCD camera captures a series of 2D projection images, either the attenuated
intensity of light (transmission mode) or the amount of emitted photons (emission mode).
These projections encode indirect information about the 3D internal structure of the sample, and
a reconstruction algorithm is required to recover the volumetric image.

In OPT, light passes through the specimen in approximately straight lines
which _project_ the 3D sample onto a 2D detector plane {cite:p}`Sharpe2002`.
The mathematical tool to describe such a straight-ray projection is the Radon transform
{cite:p}`natterer_mathematics_2001`.
The associated inverse problem is to reconstruct the 3D volume from its set of 2D projections acquired
at various spatial positions of the sample.
In the ideal scenario where the rotation axis is well aligned and perpendicular to the optical axis,
each row of pixels on the detector plane records a sinogram of the corresponding
cross-section of the 3D sample.
Hence, 3D reconstruction reduces to reconstructing a stack of 2D transverse slices
of the sample {cite:p}`Sharpe2004, correia_accelerated_2015`.
This is usually achieved by applying the 2D filtered backprojection (FBP) algorithm,
the discrete inversion formula of the 2D Radon transform,
in a slice-by-slice manner.

## Motivation and Contribution

Despite the best effort of OPT practitioners, artifacts may appear in reconstructed images
due to a variety of physical and mechanical errors.
These artifacts can be broadly categorized by their origin.

One category is those due to physical imperfections,
including illumination, fluorescence reporters or the CCD camera.
For instance, in emission OPT, fluctuations of the illumination intensity or
the fluorescent signal decay due to photobleaching effects typically produce smear artifacts {cite:p}`Walls:05`.
During the recording of measurements, the nonlinearity of the detector response generates streak artifacts
{cite:p}`Walls:05`;
pixel variability in the CCD camera results in ring artifacts {cite:p}`Walls:05`;
non-zero background signal in emission OPT leads to bowl artifacts {cite:p}`Walls2005, Birk2011`.

Another category is those induced by mechanical imprecision.
For example, a common issue in parallel-beam tomography is misalignment of the center of rotation,
which leads to double edge and blurring effects
{cite:p}`Walls:05, Donath2006, michalek:15, Torres2021`.
Random vibration and drift during sample rotation can cause blurry reconstructions
as well {cite:p}`Ancora2017`.
Additionally, optical aberrations, such as refraction at the interface between the agarose gel and the immersion liquid
due to the refractive index mismatch, introduce artifacts
{cite:p}`JochenBirk:11, Antonopoulos:14`.

All these artifacts stem from a mismatch between the idealized OPT model and the actual physical realization.
While they significantly degrade the reconstruction quality,
they can often be computationally corrected once properly identified {cite:p}`Walls:05, michalek:15`.

The existing forward models and reconstruction methods adopt different assumptions to improve
upon the Radon transform and account for finer experimental details of OPT, which shows the
importance of refined physical models.
It is noteworthy that these methods reconstruct the 3D sample slice by slice.
While this approach is effective in correcting some artifacts, it may
be insufficient to account for mechanical errors in the system that requires 3D representations
of the imaging geometry.

In this chapter, we follow the spirit of computational imaging and develop frameworks to
correct artifacts in OPT.
We focus on two primary sources:

- Mechanical errors in the imaging system (mechanical artifacts)
- Refractive index mismatch between the sample and the immersion liquid (optical artifacts)

For the mechanical errors, we follow the general framework introduced in [](#intro-modeling-and-solving-inverse-problems)
to model system parameters (rotations and translations)
and propose a multiscale joint reconstruction-calibration algorithm to correct artifacts.
For optical artifacts, we derive a cone-beam model based on ray optics to approximate the effect of
refractive index mismatch and introduce a fast correction technique.
We validate both methods on simulated and experimental datasets provided by our collaborators
from the Computational Bioimaging Group at [Idiap](https://www.idiap.ch/) and the
[University of Applied Sciences of Western Switzerland](https://www.unige.ch/gap/biophotonics/former-members/cedric_schmidt).
