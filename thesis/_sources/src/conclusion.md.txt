# Conclusion

In this thesis, we have applied physics-based modeling to solve inverse problems in
three representative modalities that push the limits of optical imaging.

Optical projection tomography (OPT) and photo-switching photoacoustic imaging (PAI) go beyond the optical
diffusion limit for large imaging depth.
OPT adopts optical clearing to reduce scattering while
PAI capitalizes on the photoacoustic effect and low ultrasonic scattering,
both enabling deep-tissue imaging at high resolution.
Combined with photo-switching proteins, PAI further enhances its sensitivity and specificity.

Single molecule localization microscopy (SMLM), such as STED and MINFLUX,
overcomes the optical diffraction limit for
resolution as fine as a few nanometers via the design and engineering of their point spread function (PSF).

While algorithms developed in this thesis are tailored to specific modalities,
they embody the spirit of computational imaging and share a common methodology
with the following steps:

- Understand principles of the modality by interacting closely with practitioners in the field;
- Describe the imaging system with a sufficiently accurate and efficient forward-imaging model;
- Formulate tasks as mathematical inverse problems, typically in the form of optimization problems;
- Deploy fast (iterative) algorithms to obtain a solution.

In each modality, the inverse problem to solve introduces different challenges.
In OPT, reconstructed images may suffer from artifacts due to mismatches between the forward model and 
actual experimental realization, in particular, mechanical errors and the refractive index mismatch.
Tasks there have been to characterize artifacts, propose tailored forward models for specific types of
artifacts, and design fast and efficient correction algorithms.
In photo-switching PAI, we have developed a mathematical framework that accurately models physical
processes during a photo-switching experiment and proposed a regularized unmixing algorithm to disentangle
different species of protein reporters for PA tomography and mesoscopy.
For SMLM, the focus has been engineering and computation of PSFs: first, a study to investigate the optimal
excitation patterns in MINFLUX; then, the development of a unifying theory to model and compute PSFs
in the broader context of SMLM.

## Contributions

### Correction of Artifacts in Optical Projection Tomography

In [Chapter 4](#chapter-opt), we have provided a systematic characterization and fast-and-efficient correction methods
of two specific types of artifacts that our collaborators have observed:

- mechanical artifacts due to imperfect instrumentation of sample placement and rotation stage (with the Computational Bioimaging Group at [Idiap](https://www.idiap.ch/));
- optical artifacts caused by the mismatch of refractive indices between the sample and immersion liquid
(with the [University of Applied Sciences of Western Switzerland](https://www.unige.ch/gap/biophotonics/former-members/cedric_schmidt)).

For [mechanical artifacts](#opt-mechanical), we have introduced a 3D imaging model that enables us to describe the geometry
and characterize possible mechanical errors of the system.
We have summarized various types of mechanical artifacts in a catalog, along with detailed explanation of their cause,
related correction methods, and visual clues, to help OPT practitioners calibrate their system.
We have presented a multiscale joint reconstruction-calibration algorithm tailored for large-sized OPT datasets.
Validated on phantom and experimental datasets, our framework is able to
recover unknown system parameters and yield distortion-free volumetric reconstructions.

For [optical artifacts](#opt-optical) due to refractive index mismatch between the sample and immersion liquid,
the standard parallel-beam model is inaccurate for refracted light paths.
We have proposed a ray-optics-based cone-beam model that better describes this effect
and a corresponding fast correction method via the well-established FDK algorithm for cone-beam geometry.
We have also introduced a reliable method for automatic determination of the origin of the cone beam, a parameter
required by the reconstruction, to circumvent practical difficulties in applying the algorithm.
Our method has successfully mitigated the _seagull_-shaped artifacts observed in the reconstructed images
of fluorescent beads.

### Temporal Unmixing for Photo-Switching Photoacoustic Imaging

In [Chapter 5](#chapter-pa), we have collaborated with the Helmholtz Zentrum München within the European consortium
[_SWOPT_](https://swopt.munichimaging.eu/).
We have developed a model-based temporal unmixing framework, tailored to the consortium's hardware and
novel photo-switching proteins, in two parts.

In [Part I](#pat1), we have carefully modeled the dynamics of photo-switching signals based on the underlying physics
(impact of light fluence and properties of proteins), and designed an unmixing algorithm to disentangle different
species of reporters from pre-reconstructed PA images.
For robustness against noise and clean unmixing results, we have adopted a combination of {math}`\ell_1`
(on the number of species) and total-variation (on space) regularization.
Our approach aggregates information from neighboring pixels and achieves
more coherent intensity spatial maps as compared to existing pixelwise approaches.
Validated on various phantoms and experiments, our unmixing approach enables efficient quantitative analysis
of temporally multiplexed PA signals for photo-switching PA tomography.

In [Part II](#pat2), building on Part I, we have included the acoustic model, leading to a global unmixing framework for
photo-switching PA mesoscopy which consists of widefield illumination from a fixed laser
and a linear array of ultrasound transducers.
Our acoustic model follows principles of the synthetic aperture focusing technique (originally from ultrasound imaging),
and accounts for transducer responses.
We have joined photo-switching and acoustic forward operators as one composite operator,
enabling one-step regularized reconstruction.
This approach allows us to recover concentration maps directly from acoustic signals.
Validated on numerous configurations, it has demonstrated robustness in terms of fluence estimation
and measurement noise, as compared to two-step or unregularized methods.
Computationally, we have efficiently implemented forward models as matrix-free operators,
which has circumvented memory issues in other model-based approaches and enabled fast iterative algorithms.

### Engineering of Point Spread Functions for Localization Microscopy

In [Chapter 6](#lm-chapter), we have explored engineering and precise computation of PSFs.

First, we have investigated the optimality of two excitation patterns,
four donut beams and two pairs of half-moon beams, for MINFLUX
(in collaboration with the [Vienna BioCenter](https://www.imp.ac.at/groups/francisco-balzarotti)).
We have designed a fast and cost-efficient computational optimization pipeline that comprises models of
light propagation, MINFLUX detection, and localization precision quantification via the Cramér-Rao bound.
We have shown that the donut-based pattern is optimal when the four beams are constrained to the same shape,
while two pairs of half-moon pattern further improves the precision by approximately twofold.
Additionally, we have provided a mathematical analysis of the optimality and underlying physical intuitions.

Then, we have viewed the computation of PSFs in the broader context of localization microscopy
and introduced a unifying theory for a systematic modeling and computation of PSFs.
We have shown that existing PSF models (based on the Richards-Wolf model) are in fact two separate parameterizations
of the same propagation integral,
which has enabled us to generalize diverse correction factors and apply them to both models.
Eventually, the choice of models is simplified to scalar versus vectorial cases
with Cartesian or spherical parameterization, optionally adding correction factors on top.
To complement our theoretical framework, we have developed _PSF Generator_, an PyTorch-based open-source library
that efficiently implements these models, which makes precise computation of PSFs more accessible to the microscopy
community and beyond.

## Outlook

In [Chapter 4](#chapter-opt), we have proposed a 3D model to characterize mechanical artifacts and a multiscale
joint reconstruction-calibration algorithm to correct them.
Our approach is applicable to both transmission and emission OPT, and it can be further adapted to
calibrate similar rotation-based tomographic imaging systems, such as X-ray CT.
It is also possible to include the PSF of the system in our forward model to account for out-of-focus blur, a common
issue for widefield OPT systems.
Another potential improvement is introducing regularization, such as total-variation, to the final reconstruction
step to enhance the image quality.

For optical artifacts in emission OPT,
we presented a cone-beam model to approximate refractive index mismatch at the interface of
the cylinder agarose gel and immersion liquid, which we interpret as a virtual lens.
This model can be extended to take into account the refractive index mismatch
on both sides of the sample in transmission OPT.
The cone-beam reconstruction method to correct _seagull_ artifacts was validated on fluorescent beads.
It could be applied to non-pointwise samples, for example by introducing a few fiducial markers to
calibrate the center of the cone beam automatically.

In [Chapter 5](#chapter-pa), we have developed a model-based temporal unmixing framework for photo-switching PAT and PAM.
The unmixing algorithm solves a regularized optimization problem using sparsity-based classical regularizers.
Our approach may potentially be improved by including neural-network-based regularizers.
In our framework, we adopted a linear photo-switching forward model by assuming a time-independent fluence.
A future direction is to extend it to a nonlinear model by incorporating time-dependent fluence to
the switching forward model.
This is useful when light fluence varies substantially during switching.
A possible unmixing approach in this case is to solve a joint unmixing and fluence estimation problem.
Additionally, our acoustic model is designed to incorporate further transducer properties
(simulated or measured) to enhance the quality of acoustic reconstruction for customized setups.
Lastly, our global framework could be extended to 3D photo-switching PAM.

In [Chapter 6](#lm-chapter), we first investigated optimal excitation patterns in MINFLUX via a computational framework
that includes a scalar Fourier light propagation model.
It could be replaced with a more precise vectorial model (e.g. vectorial Cartesian model in our
unifying framework of PSFs) for high-NA systems.
A future direction is the extension of our optimization framework to 3D localization, by adopting a 3D propagation model.

We then introduced a unifying theory to model and compute PSFs for localization microscopy,
and published a complementary open-source _PyTorch_ library.
The theoretical model can be extended beyond single-pass focusing to incorporate the physics of the particle emission
and accommodate dedicated models for specific microscopes, such as confocal and two-photon microscopy.
Finally, our library should be updated regularly to incorporate newest theoretical and technical advancements. 

In summary, we have shown that the framework of physics-based modeling, tailored regularization,
modern computational tools, and interdisciplinary collaboration has helped practitioners
overcome challenges in their modalities and improve image quality for their experiments.
While machine learning offers powerful tools for biomedical imaging, there also remains a fundamental need for robust,
physics-grounded reconstruction algorithms, designed for physical systems to address practical questions,
that ensure reliability and interpretability in biological applications.
By addressing the reconstruction stage of the imaging pipeline, this thesis also lays groundwork for
subsequent image analysis tasks that extract further biological information and unlock deeper biological insights.
We hope that the work conducted in this thesis supports ongoing biological research and future physics-informed learning methods.
